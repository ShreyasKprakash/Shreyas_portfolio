01

RADAX structural coupler

FRoDo-M sounding rocket · TUDSaT e.V. · sole designer
Problem

The coupler is the single flight-critical joint where the motor mount, airframe tube and avionics bay ring frame all meet. It has to transfer thrust and bending between three subsystems built by three different sub-teams, survive launch loads, and still be something a student team can assemble and disassemble by hand between test campaigns.

What I did
  • Derived the load cases and interface requirements from the three adjoining subsystems, then traded concepts against mass, part count and assembly time.
  • Modelled the coupler in Fusion 360 and produced the drawings for manufacture.
  • Ran static and transient FEM in Ansys Workbench on the sub-assembly; documented margins of safety for design sign-off.
  • Printed prototypes in AlSi10Mg by laser powder bed fusion, measured them for dimensional accuracy against the drawing, and tested mechanical performance before releasing to full-scale production.
Outcome

Flew on FRoDo-M at the European Rocketry Challenge, where the rocket reached 2,993.7 m against a 3,000 m target and the team placed 3rd. The coupler came back intact and was inspected post-flight.

02

Filament-wound composite airframe

FRoDo-M body tube · TUDSaT e.V.
Problem

The airframe had to be light enough to reach 3 km, stiff enough to survive the launch rail and motor loads, and manufacturable in-house on a filament winder by a student team with one shot at getting the layup right.

What I did
  • Designed the CFRP tube and its layup, then wound it on the mandrel and took it through cure and finishing.
  • Validated the structure with static and transient FEM in Ansys Workbench, including the interfaces to the coupler and fin can.
  • Documented margins of safety and released the assembly for manufacture.
What I took from it

Composite hardware punishes an analysis that ignores how the part gets made. Winding angle, overlap at the ends and cure handling changed the part more than any tweak to the model did — which is the same lesson I now meet daily on tank shells.

03

Owning a structures subsystem end to end

Hyacinth hybrid sounding rocket · TUDSaT e.V. · EuRoC 2026
Problem

Hyacinth is the team's first student-researched-and-developed hybrid engine vehicle, with a new recovery concept and newly developed modular avionics. Almost every interface was unproven, and three subsystems needed to converge on hardware that could be built in time to fly.

What I did
  • Own the Structures subsystem: body tube, fin can, bulkheads, couplings and release interfaces, from requirements through to manufacturing sign-off.
  • Act as Systems Engineer for Structures — write the requirements, derive the interfaces, and run Verification & Validation against them.
  • Act as Integration Engineer across propulsion, avionics and recovery: mechanical fit, packaging into the airframe, and interface control between teams.
  • Designed a new coupling in AlSi10Mg, printed and validated it dimensionally and mechanically ahead of full-scale production.
What I took from it

Most of the real work is not the part — it is the interface, and the conversation with the team on the other side of it. Writing an interface down early is what stops two correct designs from being incompatible three months later.

04

Lattice redesign and a test campaign to back it

Additive manufacturing project · TU Darmstadt
Problem

Redesign aerospace components for laser powder bed fusion so they lose mass without losing structural performance — and prove the claim rather than asserting it from a simulation.

What I did
  • Applied DfAM: lattice structures and load-path optimisation for mass reduction at equal performance.
  • Designed the test specimens and the fixtures to hold them, then ran tensile and compression testing and analysed failure behaviour and repeatability.
  • Assessed manufacturability — material selection, achievable tolerances, support and post-processing — against aerospace qualification standards.

05

Optimising an optical test rig

M.Sc. thesis · TU Darmstadt, Reactive Flows & Diagnostics
Problem

Measure bubble-induced convection inside an electrolysis cell. The measurement chain had to be optimised and commissioned — fixturing, optical alignment, triggering and calibration — before a single trustworthy data point existed.

What I did
  • Optimised the mechanical fixturing and optical alignment, and integrated cameras, sensors and illumination into a working measurement setup.
  • Set up a LabVIEW data acquisition system with hardware triggering, then calibrated and validated the full sensor chain end to end.
  • Wrote MATLAB and Python processing pipelines and ran a parametric campaign with formal uncertainty analysis.
Why it transfers

Field hardware needs rigs and fixtures as much as it needs parts. This was six months of exactly that: making a measurement repeatable and trustworthy.

06

Cryogenic hydrogen tank hardware

Lufthansa Technik · TACOMA programme (Airbus-led, LuFo VI) · current
What I work on

Development of composite cryogenic hydrogen tanks for passenger aircraft, focused on the tank shells: taking a metallic baseline structure through to a composite design, covering interfaces, mountings, tolerances and manufacturing constraints. I work directly with manufacturing partners on design-for-manufacture, material selection and qualification requirements, and take problems found on real hardware back into the design. Alongside it I support the Hydrogen Aviation Lab, developing ground-support, handling and maintenance procedures on an A320 testbed.
